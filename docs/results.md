# What it produced

Method documentation without results is a hypothesis. This page shows what the system shipped on the real project it comes from, with names changed: parties are unnamed and record IDs are fictional. Each case names the question, the method, and the result, because the method is the part you can reuse.

## An independent bottom-up budget

**Question:** the owner's rep keeps the project budget book. Is it right?

**Method:** rebuild the total-project budget from scratch, bottom-up, using only the signed documents: the base contract, every amendment, every signed change order, the buyout record (the list of which trade contractors the builder hired, and at what price), and the trail of pay applications (the builder's monthly invoices). One coordinating agent directed forty-seven subagents across five scripted fan-outs, and every section got a separate check that tried to prove it wrong. The rep's workbook was kept out of sight during the build and compared against it only afterward, so the rebuild could not drift toward the answer it was checking.

**Result:** the independent build landed within 0.8 percent of the rep's book, and the small gap was itemized: which lines rest on the same documents read differently, and which rest on judgment with no document behind them. Read it two ways: the rep's book was good, and the owner now holds an independent check that can be rebuilt on demand. Later versions added an analysis of which claims shared evidence with the book being checked. That is the difference between confirming a number and merely agreeing with it.

## Allowance forensics

**Question:** what money is sitting inside the builder's trade contracts that the owner has a right to see move?

An allowance is a placeholder amount in a bid for work not yet priced. Once the trade is hired, it sits there until someone spends it or gives it back.

**Method:** a line-by-line ledger joining every allowance the builder embedded in its trade bid comparisons to the billing trail in each invoice's schedule of values (the line-item breakdown attached to the monthly pay application), classifying each allowance as sitting, drawn, or reconciled.

**Result:** an eight-figure pool of unspent allowances surfaced, buried inside trade contracts and invisible to anyone reading the monthly invoices alone. The ledger rebuilds each month when the new pay application lands, so "sitting" balances are tracked, not remembered.

## Reconciling a cash request

**Question:** a projection of the next six months of cash needs was headed up the chain to the people who fund the project. Is the number right?

**Method:** reconcile the projection line by line against the documents already on hand: the closed bank ledger, the certified pay applications, and the payments the projection assumed had or had not already happened.

**Result:** the projection was corrected by tens of millions of dollars before the number went up the chain, working entirely from documents already in hand. Nobody asked anyone for new information. The gap was visible the whole time, spread across documents no one had joined.

## Verifying a suspected double-charge

**Question:** does a seven-figure mechanical change order bill for work already charged somewhere else?

**Method:** build a table crossing every charge inside the change order against every other change order that touched the same work, then run seven independent reviews, each one prompted to prove a duplicate exists.

**Result:** no provable double-charge, stated with exactly what was and was not checked. The arithmetic reconciled to the penny, including the builder's contractual markup, and the live question narrowed from "the whole change order" to a specific five-figure disputed remainder. The negative finding is the product: the owner stopped fighting a clean number and pointed attention at the part that was actually contestable.

## Triaging a stale pool of pending changes

**Question:** the builder's list of pending changes holds eight figures of aged ballpark estimates. Which are real exposure, and which have just gone stale and are being treated as real?

**Method:** a neutral candidate list, flagging entries by objective signals only: age, no supporting quotes, the builder's own notes, estimates sitting above quotes already received. ([analysis-patterns.md](analysis-patterns.md) explains this list-not-verdict distinction, which was learned on this very deliverable.) The list refreshes against each new copy of the builder's log, with a tab showing what entered and left the pool between snapshots.

**Result:** the owner walks into each negotiation with the pool broken into named items instead of one blended scary number, and with the builder's own log doing most of the arguing.

## Reviewing an outbound letter before it ships

**Question:** the owner's advisor drafted a firm letter to the builder. Can every claim in it be defended?

**Method:** several agents fact-check every factual claim in the draft against the original documents, then leave margin comments tied to what was actually found. Claims with no support get flagged for removal, not quietly rewritten.

**Result:** the letter shipped with every assertion traceable, one table deliberately left blank where the evidence did not yet support filling it, and one comparison withdrawn entirely as unsupported. The system's job was as much stopping claims as making them.

## The recurring health report

**Question:** what does the owner's leadership read monthly?

**Method:** a recurring report stream ([architecture.md](architecture.md)): a standing wiki page carries the schedule, and each month a new frozen, dated workbook ships covering what documents are on hand, budget position, change flow, open items, and information gaps. Every figure is cited, and every tab says what was checked and what was not.

**Result:** the owner's picture of the project stopped depending on anyone's memory, and the report's list of information gaps became the standing list of what to ask for next, which quietly improved the document set month over month.
